GENERAL CONCEPTS
1. What Are Hormones?
Hormones are chemical messengers produced by specific tissues or cells. They are
usually carried through the blood (vascular system) to other tissues, where they
produce effects even at very low concentrations.
The endocrine system uses hormones to coordinate, regulate, and control
physiological processes throughout the body.
Traditional definition of a hormone
A hormone is a chemical that:
• Is produced by a specific endocrine organ or tissue
• Is transported through the blood
• Acts on a distant target organ or tissue
• Produces an effect at very low concentrations
This definition is useful in veterinary medicine, but it is not completely strict because
some substances considered hormones, such as prostaglandins and
somatomedins, are produced by many different tissues rather than by one specific
endocrine organ.
2. Other Chemical Signaling Systems
Not all chemical messengers travel through the blood. Some act mainly locally,
allowing cells to communicate with nearby cells or with themselves.
A. Paracrine effectors
• The chemical messenger is released into the interstitial fluid.
• It usually acts on nearby or adjacent cells.
• If the messenger acts on the same cell that produced it, it is called an
autocrine effector.
Remember:
• Paracrine = acts on nearby cells
• Autocrine = acts on the cell that produced it
B. Neurotransmitters
• These chemicals are used for communication:
o between neurons, or
o between neurons and their target cells
• Their action is generally limited to a short distance and a specific area of the
target cell.
C. Exocrine effectors
• These substances are released into a body cavity or duct, rather than into
the bloodstream.
• Example: substances produced by the pancreas that are released into the
gastrointestinal tract.
3. Endocrine and Nervous Systems Work Together
The endocrine system and nervous system are closely connected.
• The nervous system is mainly responsible for rapid control of body
functions.
• The endocrine system generally produces slower but longer-lasting
effects.
• Together, they coordinate many physiological processes.
Example: Milk Let-Down Reflex
The milk let-down reflex shows how the nervous and endocrine systems work
together.
Sequence:
1. Suckling occurs
→ The mammary gland is stimulated.
2. Nerve impulses are generated
→ Signals travel from the mammary gland to the hypothalamus through the spinal
tract.
3. Hypothalamic neurons are stimulated
→ Neurosecretory neurons in the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei synthesize
oxytocin.
4. Oxytocin is transported
→ It travels down the axons of these neurons.
5. Oxytocin is released into the blood
→ It is released from nerve endings in the posterior pituitary into the bloodstream.
6. Oxytocin reaches the mammary gland
→ It causes contraction of myoepithelial cells.
7. Milk is pushed out
→ Myoepithelial cells surround the alveoli, which contain the milk-secreting cells.
→ Their contraction pushes milk into the larger cisternae near the teat and then into
the teat.
Simple flow:
Suckling → Nerve impulses → Hypothalamus → Oxytocin release → Blood →
Mammary gland → Myoepithelial contraction → Milk let-down
4. Direct Interaction Between the Nervous and Endocrine Systems
The interaction between the two systems can be even more direct.
Example: Adrenal Medulla
• The endocrine cells of the adrenal medulla are directly controlled by
preganglionic neurons.
• During stress, these neurons stimulate the adrenal medulla.
• The adrenal medulla then rapidly releases its hormones into the blood.
This allows the body to respond immediately to stressful situations.

Important reproductive hormones include:

• Prolactin (PRL)
• Oxytocin
6. Signal Amplification in the Endocrine System
One important characteristic of hormones is signal amplification.
This means that a very small amount of hormone can produce a large biological
effect.
Example with steroid hormones
A single steroid hormone molecule can activate a gene.
That can lead to:
1 hormone → many mRNA molecules → many enzyme molecules
Example with protein hormones
One protein hormone molecule can stimulate the production of many cAMP
molecules.
Then:
1 hormone → many cAMP molecules → activation of many enzymes
Because of this amplification, hormones can produce strong effects even when
present in extremely small amounts.
Important concentration
Hormones can produce significant effects at plasma concentrations as low as
approximately:
10⁻¹¹ to 10⁻¹² mol
7. Hormones Can Affect Existing Enzyme Activity
Hormones do not only cause cells to make new proteins or enzymes.
They can also change the activity of enzymes that are already present in the cell.
This means that cells have some basic or basal enzyme activity even when no
hormone is present. Hormones can increase or decrease this activity.
8. Speed and Duration of Hormonal Effects
Hormonal responses are generally:
• Slower to begin
• Longer-lasting
Hormonal effects may last from:
minutes → days
This is different from the nervous system.

SYNTHESIS OF HORMONES
Hormones can be divided into several major classes:
Hormone class Examples
Proteins Growth hormone (GH), insulin, ACTH
Peptides Oxytocin, vasopressin
Amines Dopamine, melatonin, epinephrine
Steroids Cortisol, progesterone, vitamin D
The way a hormone is produced and stored depends mainly on whether it is a
protein/peptide hormone or a steroid hormone.
1. Protein and Peptide Hormone Synthesis
Basic process:
Preprohormone → Prohormone → Active hormone → Storage in granules →
Exocytosis
Protein and peptide hormones are first made as larger inactive precursor molecules
called preprohormones.
Step 1: Synthesis in ribosomes
• Hormone synthesis begins on ribosomes.
• The newly forming molecule is called a preprohormone.
• The "pre" portion directs the molecule to the rough endoplasmic reticulum
(RER).
• As the preprohormone is being made, it enters the inside of the RER.
Step 2: Formation of the prohormone
• Enzymes called peptidases in the RER quickly remove the "pre" portion.
• The remaining molecule is called a prohormone.
• The prohormone leaves the RER inside small vesicles that bud off from the
RER.
Step 3: Processing in the Golgi apparatus
• The vesicles carrying the prohormone move to the Golgi apparatus.
• They fuse with the Golgi membranes and are processed into secretory
granules.
• The prohormone is then cleaved into its active hormone.
• Most of the hormone reaches its final active form in the Golgi apparatus,
although some prohormone may remain.
Step 4: Storage
• The active protein/peptide hormone is stored in secretory granules inside
the endocrine cell.
• A small amount may be released continuously, but most is stored until a
specific signal tells the cell to release it.
Step 5: Release by exocytosis
When the appropriate signal is received:
↑ Intracellular Ca²⁺ → granules fuse with cell membrane → exocytosis → hormone
released
Exocytosis requires:
• ATP for energy
• Calcium (Ca²⁺)
The increase in cytoplasmic calcium can come from:
• Release of Ca²⁺ from the endoplasmic reticulum
• Release of Ca²⁺ from mitochondria
• Influx of Ca²⁺ from outside the cell
Easy flow to remember:
Ribosome → Preprohormone → RER removes "pre" → Prohormone → Golgi → Active
hormone → Secretory granules → Ca²⁺ + ATP → Exocytosis → Blood
Key point:
Protein and peptide hormones are made first, stored in granules, and released
when needed.
2. Steroid Hormone Synthesis
Steroid hormones are different from protein hormones.
Steroids are lipophilic (fat-soluble) and are mainly made from cholesterol.
Major steroid groups include:
Adrenocortical hormones
• Glucocorticoids
• Mineralocorticoids
Sex hormones
• Estrogens

-- 3 of 13 --

• Progesterone
• Androgens
All steroid hormones have a common four-ring, 17-carbon structure that comes
from cholesterol.
3. Where Does the Cholesterol Come From?
Most steroid-producing cells obtain cholesterol from the blood.
Main source:
Liver → cholesterol → blood → steroid-producing cell
The liver produces cholesterol, which can be transported in the blood as part of low-
density lipoproteins (LDLs).
How cholesterol enters the cell
1. LDL binds to a receptor on the cell membrane.
2. The LDL enters the cell.
3. Lysosomal enzymes break down the LDL.
4. The cholesterol is released.
The cholesterol can then either:
• Be used immediately to make steroids, or
• Be stored inside the cell as cholesterol ester.
A small amount of steroid precursor can also be synthesized within the cell from
acetate, but most steroid hormones come from cholesterol.
4. How Steroid Hormones Are Made
The first step in making all steroid hormones from cholesterol is:
Cholesterol → Pregnenolone
This occurs in the mitochondria.
After pregnenolone is formed, it undergoes additional chemical changes. These
reactions may occur:
• In the mitochondria, or
• In other parts of the cell after the steroid molecule moves there.
The exact control of steroid movement between different cell compartments is not
completely understood.
5. Different Cells Make Different Steroid Hormones
The type of steroid hormone produced depends on the enzymes present in the cell.
In other words:
The enzymes determine which steroid hormone it can make.
Example: Adrenal cortex
Cells of the adrenal cortex have specific enzymes called hydroxylases.
These enzymes are needed to modify specific carbon positions, particularly:
• Carbon 11
• Carbon 21
These reactions are essential for producing:
• Glucocorticoids
• Mineralocorticoids
6. Sex Steroid Synthesis
Sex steroid production generally follows this sequence:
Pregnenolone → Progesterone → Androgens → Estrogens
However, different cells contain different enzymes, so they cannot all produce every
type of steroid.
Example: Leydig cells of the testis
Leydig cells can:
• Make pregnenolone
• Make progesterone
• Convert progesterone into androgens
But they lack the enzymes needed to convert androgens into estrogens.
Therefore, Leydig cells mainly produce androgens, rather than estrogens.
7. Adrenal Cortex Can Also Make Sex Steroids
The adrenal cortex has enzyme systems that allow it to produce:
• Adrenocortical hormones
• Sex steroids
Normally, the adrenal cortex produces:
• Large amounts of adrenocortical hormones
• Small amounts of sex steroids
However, in certain pathological conditions, adrenal production of sex steroids can
increase.
8. Steroid Hormones Are NOT Stored
This is one of the most important differences between steroid and protein hormones.
Protein/peptide hormones:
Stored in secretory granules → released when needed
Steroid hormones:
Not stored → synthesized and released immediately
Because steroids are lipophilic, they can easily pass through the cell membrane by
simple diffusion.
Therefore:
Rate of steroid hormone secretion = rate of steroid hormone synthesis
The two processes are therefore tightly coupled.
What is stored?
Steroid-producing cells do not store large amounts of the finished hormone.
The main stored material is the precursor cholesterol, stored as cholesterol ester.

TRANSPORT OF HORMONES IN THE BLOOD
Hormones that act on distant target tissues must be transported through the
bloodstream.
The way a hormone is carried in the blood depends mainly on whether it is water-
soluble (hydrophilic) or fat-soluble (lipophilic).
1. Protein Hormones Are Hydrophilic
Protein and peptide hormones are hydrophilic, meaning they dissolve easily in
water.
Since blood plasma is mostly water, these hormones can travel through the
bloodstream in dissolved form.
Forms of protein hormones in the blood
Protein hormones may circulate as:
• Monomers = one unit
• Polymers = multiple units
For example, insulin can occur in different forms.
Some hormones that normally have several subunits may also circulate as individual
subunits.
However:
Subunit form → lower biological activity/potency
This means that separating the subunits generally makes the hormone less effective.
Key point:
Protein/peptide hormones = hydrophilic → dissolve directly in plasma → usually
do not need carrier proteins.
2. Steroid and Thyroid Hormones Are Lipophilic
Steroid hormones and thyroid hormones are lipophilic, meaning they are fat-
soluble.
Because blood is an aqueous (water-based) environment, these hormones have poor
solubility in plasma.
Therefore, they usually cannot travel freely through the blood in large amounts.
Instead, they are transported by binding to plasma proteins.
3. Types of Transport Proteins
There are two important types of protein binding involved in transporting steroid and
thyroid hormones:
A. Specific/high-affinity binding proteins
These proteins have a strong preference for particular hormones.
Example:
Transcortin
• Has a high affinity for cortisol
• Also has a high affinity for corticosterone

-- 5 of 13 --

• It can also transport progesterone, although it binds progesterone less
strongly
Important characteristic:
High affinity = strong binding but low capacity
These proteins have low capacity because they are present in relatively small
amounts in plasma.
B. Nonspecific/low-affinity binding proteins
The main example is albumin.
• Albumin binds steroid hormones with low affinity.
• However, albumin is present in very high concentrations in plasma.
Therefore:
Low affinity = weaker binding but high capacity
Albumin can carry large amounts of steroid hormones because there is so much
albumin in the blood.
4. Free Hormone vs Bound Hormone
A hormone can exist in the blood in two forms:
Bound hormone
Hormone attached to a transport protein.
Free (unbound) hormone
Hormone that is not attached to a protein.
The free form is the biologically active form.
A hormone must become free/unbound before it can enter a target cell and produce
its biological effect.
Important relationship:
Bound hormone ⇌ Free hormone
There is a continuous equilibrium between the two forms.
When free hormone is used by tissues, more bound hormone quickly dissociates
from its carrier protein and becomes free.
This allows the body to maintain a supply of active hormone.
5. How Much Hormone Is Free?
Only a small portion of a lipophilic hormone in the blood is usually free.
General rule:
About 1% of the total hormone is free
The remaining majority is bound to proteins.
Exception: Cortisol
Cortisol has a somewhat larger free fraction:
Up to about 10% of cortisol may be free.
Remember:
Most lipophilic hormone = bound
Small amount = free and biologically active
6. Why Protein Binding Is Important
Binding to carrier proteins provides several advantages:
• It allows lipophilic hormones to be transported through watery blood.
• It creates a reservoir of hormone that can quickly release free hormone.
• It helps maintain a relatively stable concentration of free hormone.
The free hormone is continually used by tissues, and the bound hormone can
dissociate quickly to replace it.
7. Measurement of Hormone Levels
For most hormones, laboratories usually measure the total hormone concentration,
meaning:
Free hormone + Bound hormone = Total hormone
Exception: Thyroid hormones
For thyroid hormones, there is often greater interest in determining the amounts of:
• Bound hormone
• Free hormone
because the relationship between these forms is especially important when
evaluating thyroid function.
8. Steroid Hormone Concentration Reflects Secretion
For steroid hormones:
Synthesis → Release → Blood concentration
Steroid hormone synthesis and secretion are tightly linked because steroids are
generally not stored in large amounts inside endocrine cells.
Also, their metabolic clearance rates are usually relatively constant.
Therefore:
Plasma steroid concentration can often give a good indication of the rate at which
the steroid is being secreted.
9. Physiological Conditions Can Change Hormone Transport
The relationship between hormone concentration and secretion can change when the
body changes the amount of hormone-binding proteins.
Example: Pregnancy in humans
During pregnancy:
• Production of estrogen increases
• Estrogen can increase the production of estrogen-binding proteins
• More hormone may therefore become bound
• This can affect the metabolism and clearance of estrogen

Thus, physiological conditions can change hormone levels not only by changing
hormone production but also by changing binding proteins and hormone
metabolism.

HORMONE-CELL INTERACTION
A hormone only affects certain cells even though it may circulate throughout the
body. This is because target cells have specific receptors that recognize and bind
the hormone.
1. Location of Hormone Receptors
The location of the receptor depends on the type of hormone.
Protein and peptide hormones
• These hormones cannot pass through the cell membrane because they are
water-soluble.
• Their receptors are located on the plasma membrane of the target cell.
Steroid hormones
• Steroids are lipid-soluble, so they can pass through the cell membrane.
• Their receptors are found inside the cell, in the:
o Cytoplasm, or
o Nucleus
Easy rule:
Protein/peptide → receptor on the cell membrane
Steroid → receptor inside the cell
2. Why Only Certain Cells Respond to Hormones
Although some hormones, especially steroids, can enter many or even most cells,
they only produce an effect in cells that have the correct receptor.
Hormone receptors have two important characteristics:
Specificity
A receptor recognizes and binds a particular hormone.
High affinity
The receptor can bind the hormone strongly, even when the hormone is present in
very small amounts in the blood.
Therefore:
Specific receptor + high affinity → small hormone concentration can still produce
a strong biological response
3. Termination of Hormone Action
A hormone's effect must eventually stop.
The most common way this happens is when the hormone dissociates (separates)
from its receptor.
Hormone-receptor binding is noncovalent, meaning the hormone and receptor are
not permanently chemically attached.
Usually:
↓ Hormone concentration in blood → more hormone leaves receptor → hormone
action decreases/stops
When the hormone concentration falls, the chemical equilibrium favors dissociation
rather than continued binding.
4. Receptor Internalization
Hormone action can also stop when the hormone-receptor complex is taken into
the cell by endocytosis.

-- 7 of 13 --

Inside the cell:
• Lysosomal enzymes break down the hormone.
• The receptor is protected because it is associated with the membrane of the
vesicle.
• The receptor can then be recycled back to the plasma membrane and used
again.
Simple sequence:
Hormone + receptor → endocytosis → hormone degraded → receptor recycled
5. Number of Receptors
Cells normally have many more receptors than are actually needed to produce a
biological response.
In fact:
Less than 50% of receptors usually need to be occupied to produce a maximal
response.
This means a cell can still respond maximally even when many receptors are
unoccupied.
6. Receptor Regulation
Although cells have excess receptors, the number of receptors can change.
Changes in receptor number mainly affect the cell's sensitivity to a hormone, rather
than its maximum possible response.
More receptors
↑ Receptors → ↑ chance of hormone-receptor interaction → ↑ sensitivity
Fewer receptors
↓ Receptors → ↓ chance of interaction → ↓ sensitivity
7. Upregulation: Increasing Receptor Numbers
A hormone can sometimes stimulate production of more receptors for a different
hormone.
Example: Ovarian follicle
During the later part of the follicular phase:
• FSH stimulates ovarian granulosa cells.
• FSH causes the cells to increase their LH receptors.
• The predominant receptor changes from FSH receptors to LH receptors.
This allows control of the ovarian follicle to shift from FSH to LH.
This change is important for:
• Ovulation
• Luteinization, which is the formation of the corpus luteum
Key concept:
FSH → increases LH receptors → follicle becomes more responsive to LH →
ovulation + luteinization
8. Downregulation: Decreasing Receptor Numbers
Continuous exposure to a hormone can cause the number of its receptors to
decrease.
This is called downregulation.
It may occur when:
• A hormone or agonist continuously stimulates the receptor.
• An agonist has a very high affinity for the receptor.
• Hormone levels become pathologically high.
Result:
Persistent high hormone exposure → ↓ receptor numbers → ↓ cell sensitivity →
hormone resistance
This can cause an animal to become less responsive or resistant to continued
hormone treatment.
POSTRECEPTOR CELL RESPONSES
After a hormone binds to its receptor, the cell must produce a response.
The mechanism depends on whether the hormone is a steroid or a protein/peptide
hormone.
9. Steroid Hormone Action
Steroid hormones can cross the plasma membrane because they are lipid-soluble.
They can then interact with receptors in the cytoplasm or nucleus.
A. Steroids with cytoplasmic receptors
Examples:
• Androgens
• Glucocorticoids
The sequence is:
Steroid enters cell → binds cytoplasmic receptor → receptor becomes activated →
complex moves into nucleus → binds DNA → mRNA produced → protein synthesis
→ biological response
Step-by-step:
1. The steroid passes through the plasma membrane.
2. It binds its specific receptor in the cytoplasm.
3. The hormone-receptor complex becomes activated.
4. The complex moves into the nucleus.
5. It binds specific regions of DNA called response elements on the chromatin.
6. This stimulates the production of messenger RNA (mRNA).
7. mRNA moves to the ribosomes.
8. Ribosomes use the mRNA to make specific proteins/enzymes.
9. These proteins produce the desired biological response.

-- 8 of 13 --

B. Steroids with nuclear receptors
Some steroids, such as estrogen, can enter the nucleus and bind directly to their
nuclear receptors.
The basic outcome is still:
Steroid → receptor → DNA response element → mRNA → protein synthesis →
biological effect
10. Why Steroid Effects Are Usually Slower
Steroid hormones generally produce effects by changing gene expression and
making new proteins.
Because protein synthesis takes time:
Steroid hormone action = relatively slow but often longer-lasting
11. Protein and Peptide Hormone Action
Protein and peptide hormones cannot cross the plasma membrane.
Therefore, they cannot directly enter the cell and interact with intracellular DNA
receptors like steroids do.
Instead, they bind to receptors on the plasma membrane.
The receptor then activates an internal signaling molecule called a second
messenger.
Basic pathway:
Hormone → membrane receptor → second messenger → intracellular
enzymes/proteins → biological response
12. cAMP: An Important Second Messenger
One of the best-known second messengers is cyclic AMP (cAMP).
Sequence:
Hormone binds receptor → activates adenylyl cyclase → cAMP increases → protein
kinases activated → cellular response
Step-by-step:
1. Protein/peptide hormone binds to its receptor on the plasma membrane.
2. This activates the enzyme adenylyl cyclase.
3. Adenylyl cyclase produces cAMP.
4. cAMP activates protein kinases.
5. Protein kinases phosphorylate proteins.
6. These changes produce the biological response.
13. Other Second Messengers
cAMP is not the only second messenger.
Important examples include:
Calcium (Ca²⁺)
• Changes in cytoplasmic calcium can act as an important intracellular signal.
• Calcium can work with proteins such as calmodulin.
IP₃ (inositol triphosphate)
• Produced from phosphatidylinositol metabolism.
• One major action of IP₃ is to stimulate the release of Ca²⁺ from intracellular
stores.
DAG (diacylglycerol)
• Also produced from phosphatidylinositol metabolism.
• Can activate phospholipase A.
• This promotes formation of arachidonic acid.
• Arachidonic acid is then used to produce prostaglandins.
Important flow:
IP₃ → ↑ intracellular Ca²⁺
DAG → phospholipase A activation → arachidonic acid → prostaglandins
14. Protein/Peptide Hormones Usually Act Faster
Protein and peptide hormones often produce a faster response than steroid
hormones.
Why?
Because they can activate enzymes and proteins that already exist inside the cell.
They usually do not need to wait for the cell to make new enzymes.
Compare:
Protein/peptide hormone:
Hormone → membrane receptor → second messenger → activates existing enzymes →
rapid response
Steroid hormone:
Hormone → intracellular receptor → DNA → mRNA → new protein synthesis → slower
response

METABOLISM OF HORMONES
Hormone activity is limited by how quickly hormones are metabolized and removed
from the body.
The metabolism process is different for steroid, thyroid, and protein hormones.
1. Steroid Hormone Metabolism
Steroid hormones are mainly metabolized in the liver.
The usual process is:
Steroid → reduction → conjugation → increased water solubility → excretion in urine
Step 1: Reduction
The steroid molecule is chemically modified, usually by reduction.
Step 2: Conjugation
The modified steroid is combined with:
• Sulfates
• Glucuronides
This process is called conjugation.
Why is conjugation important?
Steroid hormones are naturally lipid-soluble. Conjugation makes them much more
water-soluble.
Therefore:
Water-soluble steroid metabolites → easier removal → excreted in urine
Main organ:
Liver
2. Thyroid Hormone Metabolism
During metabolism of thyroid hormones, iodine molecules are removed from the
hormone.
This process is called deiodination.
So:
Thyroid hormone → removal of iodine → metabolites
3. Protein Hormone Metabolism
Protein hormones are broken down by enzymes called peptidases.
If the protein hormone contains disulfide bonds, these bonds are first reduced
before the protein is cleaved.
Basic sequence:
Disulfide bond reduction → peptidase cleavage → smaller hormone
fragments/metabolites
4. Are Hormone Metabolites Always Inactive?
Usually, a hormone metabolite is less biologically active than the original hormone.
However, this is not always true.
Some steroid metabolites or conjugates may still have significant biological activity.
Example 1: Testosterone → Dihydrotestosterone (DHT)
Testosterone → DHT
This conversion occurs inside certain tissues.
The question is whether this should be considered "metabolism" because:
• DHT is produced from testosterone.
• However, DHT is more biologically potent than testosterone.
Therefore, not every metabolic conversion makes a hormone less active.
Example 2: Estradiol → Estrone
Peripheral tissues, including adipose tissue, can convert:
Estradiol-17β → Estrone
This is considered a form of hormone metabolism.
However:
• Estrone is a natural estrogen
• It is still relatively potent biologically
Key point:
Metabolism does not always mean complete loss of biological activity.
5. Hormone Clearance
Clearance refers to how quickly a hormone is removed from the blood.
In most situations, hormone metabolism and clearance are relatively constant.

-- 10 of 13 --

Therefore, the concentration of a hormone in the blood usually reflects its rate of
synthesis/secretion.
Simple relationship:
↑ Hormone synthesis/secretion → ↑ Blood hormone concentration
↓ Hormone synthesis/secretion → ↓ Blood hormone concentration
This is because the rate at which the hormone is normally cleared tends to remain
fairly stable.
6. Factors That Can Change Hormone Clearance
Although clearance is usually relatively constant, it can change under certain
conditions.
Pregnancy
In humans, pregnancy can increase the production of hormone-binding plasma
proteins.
More hormone may become bound to these proteins, which can decrease hormone
clearance.
Liver disease
Liver disease can decrease the amount of hormone-binding proteins.
This may increase hormone clearance in some situations.
Important idea:
Changes in binding proteins can alter the rate at which hormones are cleared from
the circulation.
FEEDBACK CONTROL MECHANISMS
Hormone concentrations must be carefully controlled because the effects of
hormones depend on their concentration in the blood.
The main factor determining blood hormone concentration is generally the rate of
hormone secretion.
The body uses feedback systems to keep hormone levels within an appropriate
range.
7. Negative Feedback
Negative feedback is the most important and most common hormone control
system.
In negative feedback:
↑ Hormone concentration → ↓ further hormone production/secretion
The purpose is to prevent hormone levels from becoming too high and to help
maintain a stable internal environment.
Basic principle:
Too little hormone → increase production
Enough/too much hormone → decrease production
This creates a continuous adjustment toward the optimal hormone concentration.
8. Hypothalamus-Pituitary Negative Feedback
The hypothalamus and anterior pituitary gland are important control centers for
many endocrine systems.
When hormone levels are too low:
↓ Target gland hormone
→ hypothalamus detects the low level
→ ↑ releasing hormone
→ anterior pituitary releases more tropic hormone
→ target endocrine organ is stimulated
→ ↑ target hormone production
When hormone levels are too high:
↑ Target gland hormone
→ hypothalamus decreases releasing hormone
→ anterior pituitary decreases tropic hormone
→ target organ decreases hormone production
→ ↓ target hormone
General flow:
Low target hormone → ↑ Hypothalamic releasing hormone → ↑ Pituitary tropic
hormone → ↑ Target gland hormone
High target hormone → ↓ Hypothalamic releasing hormone → ↓ Pituitary tropic
hormone → ↓ Target gland hormone
9. Negative Feedback Is Continuous, Not "All-or-None"
The endocrine system does not simply switch hormone production completely on or
off.
Instead, hormone production is continuously adjusted.
The system constantly monitors hormone concentrations and makes small changes
to keep the level near the desired physiological set point.
Key concept:
Negative feedback = continuous adjustment toward an optimal level
10. Negative Feedback Can Also Control Physiological Substances
Negative feedback is not limited to situations where one hormone controls another
hormone.
A physiological substance can also trigger a hormonal response that brings the
substance back toward normal.
Example: Blood glucose and insulin
↑ Blood glucose → ↑ insulin secretion → glucose is reduced toward normal
Although increased glucose causes more insulin, this is still considered negative
feedback because the final effect of insulin is to decrease the original increase in
glucose.

-- 11 of 13 --

Simple idea:
Change in physiological variable → hormone response → variable moves back
toward normal
11. Positive Feedback
Positive feedback is much less common than negative feedback.
In positive feedback:
Initial stimulus → increased hormone response → stronger stimulus → even greater
hormone release
Instead of opposing the original change, the system amplifies it.
12. Example of Positive Feedback: LH Surge Before Ovulation
A classic example is the preovulatory LH surge.
Sequence:
Ovarian follicle develops
→ follicle produces increasing amounts of estrogen
→ increased estrogen stimulates the hypothalamic-pituitary system
→ LH secretion increases greatly
→ large LH surge
→ ovulation
→ the stimulus for the LH surge decreases
Thus:
↑ Estrogen → ↑ LH → ovulation → estrogen stimulus declines
The LH surge is therefore a positive-feedback response to increasing estrogen.
Important point:
The LH response does not continue forever. There is a definite endpoint:
Ovulation
The duration of the LH surge is also influenced by control mechanisms within the
hypothalamus.

ENDOCRINE SECRETORY PATTERNS
Hormones are not always released at a constant rate.
Their secretion can follow rhythmic patterns that are influenced by factors such as:
• Sleep
• Light
• Other environmental or physiological signals
13. Circadian Rhythm
A circadian rhythm is a biological rhythm that follows an approximately 24-hour
cycle.
Many daily hormone rhythms are influenced by light or darkness.
Important terminology:
Circadian rhythm
= approximately 24-hour rhythm and is the preferred general term.
Diurnal rhythm
= specifically refers to a rhythm associated with activity during the daytime.
Nocturnal rhythm
= refers to a rhythm that is active mainly at night.
Therefore, not every 24-hour rhythm should automatically be called "diurnal."
14. Ultradian Rhythm
Hormonal changes can also occur at much shorter intervals.
A rhythm occurring repeatedly within a day, often approximately every hour, is called
an:
Ultradian rhythm
Example concept:
Instead of one large hormone release each day, a hormone may be released in
repeated pulses throughout the day.
