THE THYROID GLAND
1. Location and Structure
In most mammals, the thyroid gland is located caudal to the trachea, usually
around the 1st or 2nd tracheal ring.
It is one of the most important endocrine glands for metabolic regulation.
The thyroid is made up of many circular structures called thyroid follicles.
Thyroid follicle
Each follicle consists of follicular cells arranged in a circle around a central space
called the follicular lumen.
The lumen contains a thick, homogeneous substance called colloid.
Colloid = major storage site of thyroid hormones
Follicular cell appearance
The shape of follicular cells changes depending on their activity:
• Cuboidal cells → relatively inactive/basal secretion
• Elongated cells → actively stimulated to release thyroid hormone
2. Parafollicular Cells (C Cells)
Another important thyroid cell is the:
Parafollicular cell / C cell
These cells are located outside the thyroid follicles.
They produce:
Calcitonin
Calcitonin is important in the regulation of calcium metabolism.
Remember:
Follicular cells → T3 and T4
C cells → calcitonin
THYROID HORMONE SYNTHESIS
3. Thyroid Hormones Are Made From Tyrosine and Iodine
The thyroid hormones are unusual because they are made from:
• Tyrosine
• Iodine
They are also unusual because a large amount of the finished hormone is stored
outside the cells, within the colloid.
The main thyroid hormones are:
• T4 = thyroxine / tetraiodothyronine
• T3 = triiodothyronine
They contain 4 and 3 iodine atoms, respectively.
4. Thyroglobulin
Tyrosine is part of a very large protein called:
Thyroglobulin (Tg)
Thyroglobulin is produced inside the thyroid follicular cells and then secreted into
the follicular lumen/colloid.
Therefore:
Follicular cell → thyroglobulin → colloid
The tyrosine residues in thyroglobulin provide the basic structure from which thyroid
hormones are formed.
5. Iodide Uptake
Iodine from the diet is converted to iodide in the gastrointestinal tract.
The iodide is then transported through the blood to the thyroid.
Thyroid follicular cells actively trap iodide from the blood.
Because of this active transport:
Intracellular iodide concentration can become about 25–200 times higher than
the extracellular concentration.
This allows the thyroid to collect enough iodide to make thyroid hormones.
6. Addition of Iodine to Tyrosine
Iodide moves across the apical membrane of the follicular cell toward the colloid.
It is then attached to tyrosine residues within thyroglobulin.
A tyrosine ring can receive up to 2 iodine molecules.
One iodine:
Monoiodotyrosine (MIT)
Two iodines:
Diiodotyrosine (DIT)
7. Formation of T3 and T4
The iodinated tyrosine molecules are then coupled together.
T4 formation
DIT + DIT → T4
T4 is also called:
Tetraiodothyronine / thyroxine
It contains 4 iodine atoms.
T3 formation
MIT + DIT → T3
T3 is called:
Triiodothyronine
It contains 3 iodine atoms.
Easy memory:
DIT + DIT = T4
MIT + DIT = T3
8. Thyroperoxidase

-- 1 of 17 --

An essential enzyme in thyroid hormone synthesis is:
Thyroperoxidase (TPO)
TPO works together with hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂).
It is responsible for:
• Attaching iodine to tyrosine residues of thyroglobulin
• Helping form T3 and T4
Simple pathway:
Tyrosine + iodine → MIT/DIT → coupling → T3/T4
Unique feature:
Thyroid hormones are the only hormones that contain a halogen, specifically
iodine.
THYROID HORMONE STORAGE
9. Thyroid Hormones Are Stored Outside the Cells
Most endocrine hormones are stored inside cells.
The thyroid is unusual because its hormones are stored outside the follicular cells,
in the colloid.
The hormones remain attached to thyroglobulin while they are stored.
Why is this important?
It gives the thyroid a large reserve of thyroid hormone.
This allows animals to continue producing adequate thyroid hormone for some time
even during periods of iodine deficiency.
Remember:
Most endocrine glands → store hormone inside cells
Thyroid → stores hormone outside cells in colloid
RELEASE OF THYROID HORMONES
10. How T3 and T4 Are Released
Before the thyroid can release T3 and T4, the thyroglobulin containing the hormones
must be brought back into the follicular cell.
Sequence:
Colloid thyroglobulin
→ taken into follicular cell
→ fuses with lysosomes
→ lysosomal enzymes break down thyroglobulin
→ T3 and T4 are released
→ T3 and T4 leave the cell
→ enter surrounding/interstitial fluid and then blood
11. Role of Lysosomes
The thyroglobulin-containing vesicles fuse with lysosomes inside the follicular cell.
The lysosomal enzymes cleave:
• MIT
• DIT
• T3
• T4
from thyroglobulin.
The released T3 and T4 then pass through the basal cell membrane into the
surrounding tissue and bloodstream.
Because thyroid hormones are lipid-soluble, they can freely cross the cell
membrane.
12. Recycling of Iodine and Tyrosine
The thyroid does not waste the iodine-containing building blocks.
After thyroglobulin is broken down:
MIT and DIT → deiodination → iodide + tyrosine
An enzyme called:
Iodotyrosine dehalogenase
removes iodine from MIT and DIT.
The recovered:
• Iodide
• Tyrosine
are recycled and used to make more thyroid hormone.
Important:
MIT + DIT → iodine and tyrosine recycled
T3 AND T4 PRODUCTION
13. The Thyroid Mainly Produces T4
The thyroid gland itself produces:
• Mostly T4
• Small amounts of T3
However, most of the body's T3 is produced outside the thyroid.
This happens when tissues convert T4 → T3.
14. Conversion of T4 to T3
The major tissues involved in converting T4 to T3 include:
• Liver
• Kidneys
• Muscle
The liver and kidneys have high concentrations of deiodinating enzymes.
However, because muscle makes up such a large portion of the body, it produces a
large amount of T3 overall.
The important enzyme that removes iodine from the outer ring of T4 is:
5′-monodeiodinase

-- 2 of 17 --

Reaction:
T4 → T3
T3 is an important biologically active thyroid hormone.
15. Reverse T3
T4 can also be converted into:
Reverse T3 (rT3)
In this case, iodine is removed from the inner ring instead of the outer ring.
T4 → reverse T3
Reverse T3 has very little biological activity compared with T3.
Importantly:
The thyroid gland itself does not produce reverse T3.
It is produced by extrathyroidal tissues through deiodination of T4.
T3/T4 CONVERSION SUMMARY
T4 → T3
• Iodine removed from the outer ring
• Produces biologically active T3
• Mainly occurs outside the thyroid
T4 → reverse T3
• Iodine removed from the inner ring
• Produces a form with little biological activity
• Also occurs outside the thyroid
TRANSPORT OF THYROID HORMONES IN BLOOD
16. Thyroid Hormones Are Transported Bound to Plasma Proteins
T3 and T4 are lipid-soluble, so they do not dissolve well in the water-based plasma.
Therefore, most thyroid hormones travel in the blood attached to plasma binding
proteins.
There is considerable species variation in the proteins that transport thyroid
hormones.
17. Main Thyroid Hormone-Binding Proteins
There are three important plasma proteins:
A. Thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG)
TBG is generally the most important specific carrier.
It:
• Has high affinity for T4
• Also carries T3
• Has relatively low capacity because its concentration in plasma is low
Important veterinary point:
TBG has been found in domestic animals except cats.
B. Albumin
Albumin:
• Has low affinity for T3 and T4
• Has high capacity
Why?
Because albumin is present in very large amounts in plasma.
Therefore, albumin can transport a large amount of thyroid hormone despite its
weaker binding.
In the absence of TBG:
Albumin becomes the most important carrier.
This is particularly relevant in species such as the cat, which lacks TBG.
C. Thyroxine-binding prealbumin
A third transport protein is:
Thyroxine-binding prealbumin
It:
• Specifically binds T4
• Has intermediate affinity and capacity
• Falls between TBG and albumin in its binding characteristics
The term "prealbumin" refers to how the protein moves during electrophoresis. It
does not mean that it is a precursor used to make albumin.
18. Free vs Bound Thyroid Hormone
Most T3 and T4 in the blood is protein-bound.
Only a very small amount is free.
The free hormone is the biologically available form that can leave the blood and
interact with target cells.
Human values:
• Free T4 ≈ 0.03%
• Free T3 ≈ 0.3%
Dog values:
Dogs have a somewhat larger free fraction because their plasma proteins have a
lower affinity for thyroid hormones.
• Free T4 = slightly <1%
• Free T3 = slightly >1%
Key point:
Bound hormone = mainly transport/storage reservoir
Free hormone = available to interact with target tissues
19. Equilibrium Between Free and Bound Hormone

-- 3 of 17 --

The amount of free and bound thyroid hormone can change depending on
physiological or drug-related conditions.
There is a continuous equilibrium:
Bound hormone ⇌ Free hormone
When free hormone is used by tissues, more hormone can be released from the
protein-bound pool.
The body rapidly adjusts this balance to maintain an appropriate concentration of free
hormone.
20. Factors That Can Change Free Thyroid Hormone Levels
The free/bound balance can be affected by:
• Illness
• Drugs
• Changes in plasma-binding proteins
• Increased estrogen, such as during pregnancy
When binding changes, the body can compensate by:
• Reducing hormone metabolism, and/or
• Increasing thyroid hormone production through TSH stimulation
These responses help maintain an appropriate amount of free thyroid hormone.

THYROID HORMONE METABOLISM, ACTIONS, AND REGULATION
1. Main Ways Thyroid Hormones Are Metabolized
The main way thyroid hormones are broken down is by removing iodine
(deiodination).
Another, less common pathway is conjugation with sulfates and glucuronides,
mainly in the liver and kidneys.
A third pathway involves changing the alanine part of the hormone through:

-- 4 of 17 --

• Transamination
• Decarboxylation
2. Deiodination
Deiodination = removal of iodine from thyroid hormones.
The enzymes involved in producing T3 and reverse T3 also help break down thyroid
hormones:
• 5′-deiodinase
• 5-deiodinase
These enzymes can remove iodine from either the 3 or 5 position of the hormone's
phenolic rings.
Important tissues for thyroid hormone breakdown:
• Skeletal muscle
• Liver
• Kidneys
Biological activity of deiodinated products
Most deiodinated thyroid hormone products have little or no important metabolic
activity.
The major exception is:
T4 → T3
T3 is biologically active and is an important thyroid hormone.
3. Conjugation
Thyroid hormones can also be inactivated by attaching:
• Sulfates
• Glucuronides
These conjugates are produced mainly in:
• Liver
• Kidneys
However:
Conjugation is less common than deiodination as a pathway for thyroid hormone
metabolism.
4. Other Metabolic Pathways
Another less important pathway changes the alanine portion of thyroid hormones.
This can happen through:
• Transamination
• Decarboxylation
These reactions contribute to further breakdown and inactivation of thyroid
hormones.
5. Excretion of Thyroid Hormone Metabolites
Most metabolized thyroid hormones are eliminated through the urine.
Mainly in urine:
• Deiodinated forms
• Conjugated forms
However, some unmetabolized thyroid hormones are secreted into bile and
eventually eliminated in the feces.
The conjugates in the intestine can then be broken down, releasing iodide.
The iodide can be absorbed again and returned to the body through the:
Enterohepatic cycle
Simple flow:
Thyroid hormone → liver metabolism → bile → intestine → iodide released →
reabsorbed → returned to circulation
6. Iodide Recycling: Humans vs Dogs
Humans are more efficient than dogs at recovering iodide through:
• Intrathyroidal recycling
• Enterohepatic recycling
This means humans are generally better at conserving and reusing iodine.
HALF-LIFE OF THYROID HORMONES
7. Thyroid Hormones Have Long Half-Lives in Humans
Thyroid hormones remain in the body much longer than many other hormones.
In humans:
T3 → half-life ≈ 1 day
T4 → half-life ≈ 6–7 days
Most other hormones have half-lives of only minutes to hours.
8. Why T4 Lasts Longer Than T3
One important reason thyroid hormones have long half-lives is that much of them is
bound to plasma proteins.
Protein binding protects the hormones from being rapidly broken down.
T4 has a longer half-life than T3 because:
T4 binds more tightly to plasma proteins → less free T4 → slower degradation →
longer half-life
9. Important Veterinary Species Difference
The half-life of T4 is much shorter in some domestic animals.
In:
• Dogs
• Cats
T4 has a half-life of less than 24 hours.
This is much shorter than the human T4 half-life of 6–7 days.

ACTIONS OF THYROID HORMONES
10. Thyroid Hormones Control Basal Metabolism
Thyroid hormones are considered the primary regulators of basal metabolic
activity.
They are important for the normal metabolic activity of almost all tissues.
Because thyroid hormones affect many tissues simultaneously, their effects are
difficult to separate into completely independent functions.
11. How Thyroid Hormones Act on Cells
Although thyroid hormones are made from amino acids, they behave much like lipid-
soluble hormones.
They can:
Cross the cell membrane → enter the cell → interact with intracellular receptors
Their main action is believed to involve the nucleus, where they influence the
transcription of mRNA.
Thyroid hormone receptors have also been identified on mitochondria, suggesting
that thyroid hormones may have some direct effects there as well.
12. Calorigenic Effect
One of the most important actions of thyroid hormones is increasing:
• Oxygen consumption
• Heat production
This is called the:
Calorigenic effect
Simple relationship:
↑ Thyroid hormone → ↑ oxygen consumption → ↑ heat production
The mitochondria are one important site involved in this effect.
CARBOHYDRATE METABOLISM
13. Effects on Carbohydrates
Thyroid hormones affect carbohydrate metabolism in several ways.
They:
• Increase intestinal absorption of glucose
• Help glucose enter muscle and fat cells
• Make cells more responsive to insulin-mediated glucose uptake
Effect on glycogen
Small amounts of thyroid hormone can:
↑ Glycogen formation
Higher amounts can promote:
↑ Glycogenolysis
where glycogen is broken down to release glucose.
PROTEIN METABOLISM AND GROWTH
14. Role in Growth and Development
Thyroid hormones work together with growth hormone (GH) and are essential for:
• Normal growth
• Normal development
Part of their effect is due to increasing:
• Amino acid uptake by tissues
• Activity of enzymes involved in protein synthesis
Thus:
Thyroid hormone + GH → normal growth and development
LIPID METABOLISM
15. Effects on Lipids
Thyroid hormones affect many aspects of fat metabolism, with an important effect
on:
Lipolysis = breakdown of fat
They also tend to decrease plasma cholesterol.
This appears to happen because thyroid hormones:
1. Increase cellular uptake of LDLs
2. Increase breakdown of LDL and cholesterol
Important clinical connection:
Thyroid hormone deficiency → decreased cholesterol clearance →
hypercholesterolemia
Therefore:
Hypercholesterolemia is a characteristic finding of thyroid hormone deficiency.
16. Catabolic Effects
The effects of thyroid hormones on:
• Carbohydrates
• Proteins
• Lipids
are often described as catabolic, especially when thyroid hormone activity is
excessive.
"Catabolic" means promoting the breakdown or use of stored nutrients.
NERVOUS SYSTEM EFFECTS
17. Thyroid Hormones and the Nervous System
Thyroid hormones are important for the normal development of the nervous
system.
This is especially important during:
• Fetal development

-- 6 of 17 --

• Neonatal development
Insufficient thyroid hormone during development can impair mental and nervous
system development.
In adults, inadequate thyroid hormone can be associated with:
• Mental dullness
• Lethargy
• Reduced mental activity
Therefore, adequate thyroid hormone is important for normal CNS function
throughout life.
18. Thyroid Hormones and the Sympathetic Nervous System
Thyroid hormones increase the effects of the sympathetic nervous system.
They do this partly by increasing the number or activity of:
β-adrenergic receptors
These receptors respond to catecholamines such as:
• Epinephrine
• Norepinephrine
Therefore:
↑ Thyroid hormone → ↑ β-adrenergic responsiveness → stronger sympathetic
effects
CARDIOVASCULAR EFFECTS
19. Effects on the Heart
Thyroid hormones increase:
• Heart rate
• Force of cardiac contraction
This occurs partly because they increase the heart's responsiveness to
catecholamines.
They promote the expression of β-adrenergic receptors, making cardiac tissue more
responsive to sympathetic stimulation.
20. Effects on Blood Pressure
Thyroid hormones can increase:
Systolic blood pressure
while generally causing little change in:
Diastolic blood pressure
This results in:
↑ Cardiac output
Thyroid hormone activity is therefore important for maintaining normal:
• Cardiac contraction
• Cardiac function
• Transmission of nerve impulses in cardiac tissue
These effects are especially obvious when thyroid hormone levels are excessively
high.
THYROID HORMONES AND DEVELOPMENT
21. Amphibian Metamorphosis
A classic demonstration of thyroid hormone action comes from amphibians.
Normal:
Thyroxine → tadpole develops into frog
Without a thyroid:
Thyroidectomy → tadpole remains large and does not normally undergo
metamorphosis
This dramatic effect is particularly characteristic of amphibians.
In other animals, thyroid hormones do not cause such obvious metamorphosis, but
they are still important for many subtle developmental and differentiation
processes.
22. Overall Role of Thyroid Hormones
Although thyroid hormone effects are often described based on what happens when
hormone levels are:
• Too low
• Too high
it is better to remember that thyroid hormones are fundamentally needed for the
normal metabolic activity of almost every tissue in the body.
REGULATION BY TSH
23. TSH Is the Main Regulator of the Thyroid Gland
Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), also called thyrotropin, is the major hormone
controlling thyroid gland activity.
TSH stimulates the thyroid through a signaling pathway involving:
TSH receptor → cAMP → protein kinase activation → thyroid gland stimulation
This promotes thyroid hormone synthesis and release.
24. Negative Feedback of Thyroid Hormones
Thyroid hormones regulate their own production through negative feedback.
When T3 and T4 levels increase:
↑ T3/T4 → ↓ TRH from hypothalamus → ↓ TSH from anterior pituitary → ↓ thyroid
activity
When thyroid hormone levels decrease:
↓ T3/T4 → less negative feedback → ↑ TRH → ↑ TSH → ↑ thyroid activity
Therefore, the system continuously adjusts thyroid hormone production to maintain
appropriate levels.

THYROID DISORDERS
1. Goitrogens and Goiter
What is a goiter?
A goiter is an enlargement of the thyroid gland that usually develops when the thyroid
cannot produce enough thyroid hormone.
One major cause is iodine deficiency.
Iodine deficiency
If the diet does not provide enough iodine:
↓ Iodine → ↓ thyroid hormone synthesis → thyroid stimulation increases → thyroid
enlarges → goiter
In many areas, iodine deficiency has been greatly reduced by using iodized salt.
2. Goitrogenic Plants and Compounds
Some plants contain substances called goitrogens, which interfere with thyroid
hormone production.
Important examples include cruciferous plants:
• Cabbage
• Kale
• Rutabaga
• Turnip
• Rapeseed
These plants contain progoitrin, which is converted to goitrin in the digestive tract.
Goitrin
Goitrin interferes with the organic binding of iodine during thyroid hormone
synthesis.
Other goitrogenic feeds contain thiocyanates.
Thiocyanates
Thiocyanates interfere with the thyroid's ability to trap/actively take up iodide.
Therefore:
Thiocyanates → ↓ iodide uptake
Goitrin → ↓ organic binding of iodine
Both can reduce thyroid hormone production and potentially cause goiter and
hypothyroidism.
3. Effect of Excess Iodine
Increasing dietary iodine can sometimes help overcome the effects of thiocyanates.
However, excess iodine is much less effective against goitrin.
So:
Extra iodine → may partially overcome thiocyanate effects
Extra iodine → has less effect against goitrin
4. Antithyroid Drugs
Understanding how goitrogens interfere with thyroid hormone synthesis helped lead
to the development of drugs used to treat hyperthyroidism.
The most potent group mentioned is the:
Thiocarbamides
• Thiourea
• Thiouracil
Other antithyroid compounds include:
• Sulfonamides
• p-Aminosalicylic acid
• Phenylbutazone
• Chlorpromazine
These compounds reduce thyroid hormone production through interference with
thyroid hormone synthesis.
HYPOTHYROIDISM IN DOGS
5. Canine Hypothyroidism
Hypothyroidism is one of the most important endocrine diseases in dogs.
The most common cause of primary hypothyroidism is:
Lymphocytic thyroiditis
6. Causes of Hypothyroidism
A. Primary hypothyroidism
The problem is directly within the thyroid gland.
Common or possible causes include:
• Lymphocytic thyroiditis
• Thyroid dysgenesis = abnormal development of the thyroid
• Dyshormonogenesis = abnormal thyroid hormone production
• T4 transport defects

-- 9 of 17 --

• Goitrogens
• Rarely, iodine deficiency
B. Secondary hypothyroidism
The problem is at the level of the pituitary gland.
Possible causes include:
• Pituitary tumors
• Radiation therapy
• Glucocorticoid exposure
Glucocorticoids may be either:
• Produced by the body (endogenous), or
• Given as medication (exogenous)
C. Tertiary hypothyroidism
The problem occurs at the level of the hypothalamus.
It may be:
• Acquired, such as from hypothalamic tumors
• Congenital, due to defects involving TRH or the TRH receptor
Easy way to remember:
Primary = thyroid problem
Secondary = pituitary problem
Tertiary = hypothalamus problem

CLINICAL SIGNS OF CANINE HYPOTHYROIDISM
8. General Clinical Signs
The onset is usually:
Slow and subtle
The most common signs are:
• Lethargy
• Obesity
9. Skin and Hair Changes
Skin problems are among the most common findings after lethargy and obesity.

Symmetrical truncal or tail-head alopecia

• Thickened skin

• Bradycardia

CLINICOPATHOLOGICAL FINDINGS
15. Anemia
Approximately 25–30% of hypothyroid dogs may have anemia.
Possible causes include:
• ↓ Erythropoietin
• ↓ Bone marrow activity
• ↓ Serum iron
• ↓ Iron-binding capacity
The anemia is usually mild and may be normocytic and normochromic.
16. Hypercholesterolemia
Approximately 75% of hypothyroid dogs develop:
Hypercholesterolemia
This occurs because of altered lipid metabolism, including:
• ↓ Fecal cholesterol excretion
• ↓ Conversion of lipids to bile acids
Important connection:
Hypothyroidism → ↓ lipid clearance → ↑ blood cholesterol
17. Hyponatremia
Mild hyponatremia has been reported in about 30% of hypothyroid dogs in one
study.
Possible mechanisms include:
• Increased total body water
• Reduced renal excretion of water
• Retention of water associated with hydrophilic deposits in tissues
18. Increased Creatine Phosphokinase
An unusual laboratory finding is:
↑ Serum creatine phosphokinase (CK/CPK)
This may be related to hypothyroid myopathy.
DIAGNOSIS OF CANINE HYPOTHYROIDISM
19. Main Diagnostic Tests
Diagnosis should not depend on one test alone.
It should combine:
• Signalment
• History
• Physical examination
• Clinicopathological findings
• Thyroid function tests
Important thyroid tests include:
• Total T4 (TT4)
• Total T3 (TT3)
• Free T4 (FT4)
• Endogenous canine TSH
20. Why T4 Results Can Be Misleading
Many factors can affect serum T4.
These include:
• Age
• Breed
• Environmental temperature
• Body temperature
• Diurnal rhythm
• Obesity
• Malnutrition
• Concurrent illness

-- 11 of 17 --

• Drugs
Breed example
Sighthounds may naturally have lower TT4 and FT4 than other dogs.
Obesity
Obese dogs may have mildly increased TT4.
Puppies
Puppies may have TT4 concentrations approximately:
2–5 times higher than adult dogs
There is also an age-related decrease in:
• Serum TT4
• Response to TSH stimulation
21. Euthyroid Sick Syndrome
Euthyroid sick syndrome occurs when a dog has another illness that causes thyroid
hormone changes even though the thyroid gland itself is not truly failing.
Typical changes include:
↓ TT4
and often:
↑ reverse T3
Diseases that can produce this pattern include:
• Diabetes mellitus
• Chronic renal failure
• Hepatic insufficiency
• Infections
Important:
A low TT4 does not automatically mean hypothyroidism.
Other illnesses must be considered.
22. Drugs That Can Lower TT4
Several drugs can decrease basal TT4 concentrations, including:
• Anesthetics
• Phenobarbital
• Primidone
• Diazepam
• Trimethoprim-sulfa
• Quinidine
• Phenylbutazone
• Salicylates
• Glucocorticoids
Therefore, medication history is important when interpreting thyroid tests.
23. Free T4
Free T4 (FT4) means the T4 that is not bound to plasma proteins.
In human medicine, FT4 is useful for distinguishing:
True hypothyroidism vs euthyroid sick syndrome
The preferred method is:
Equilibrium dialysis = gold standard
Other methods include:
Analog immunoassays
In humans, one FT4 measurement has approximately 90% diagnostic accuracy.
In dogs, early studies suggested:
• FT4 by equilibrium dialysis ≈ 90% accurate
• Other FT4 assays were no better than TT4
However, glucocorticoids can decrease both FT4 and TT4 in dogs.
24. Endogenous TSH
Endogenous TSH helps evaluate the feedback relationship between the thyroid,
pituitary, and hypothalamus.
In primary thyroid failure:
↓ T4/FT4 → pituitary detects low thyroid hormone → ↑ TSH
Therefore:
Low FT4 + high TSH → strongly suggests primary hypothyroidism
The increase in TSH becomes especially pronounced as FT4 decreases, making TSH a
sensitive indicator of early thyroid failure.
Important veterinary limitation
TSH should not be used alone to evaluate thyroid function in animals.
Approximately:
20–30% of hypothyroid dogs can have normal TSH concentrations
even though T3 and T4 are decreased.
25. Thyroglobulin Autoantibody (TgAA)
The antithyroglobulin autoantibody test (TgAA) can detect evidence of autoimmune
thyroid disease.
It is useful for:
• Identifying thyroid pathology before obvious hypothyroidism develops
• Detecting dogs with hereditary thyroid disease
• Screening dogs before breeding
26. TSH Stimulation Test
For many years, the TSH stimulation test was considered the gold standard for
diagnosing canine hypothyroidism.
However, it has important limitations.
It does not reliably distinguish:

-- 12 of 17 --

• Early hypothyroidism
• Euthyroid sick syndrome
It also does not identify:
• Secondary hypothyroidism
• Tertiary hypothyroidism
Another major problem is that exogenous bovine TSH is no longer commercially
available.
27. Other Thyroid Tests
Other tests include:
• TRH stimulation test
• Thyroid scan/scintigraphy
• Thyroid biopsy
However, each has disadvantages such as:
• Expense
• Inaccuracy
• Invasiveness
28. Practical Diagnostic Approach
A practical approach described in the notes is:
First:
Measure:
TT4 + TSH
Then:
Measure:
FT4 by equilibrium dialysis
Interpretation
All 3 abnormal:
TT4 abnormal + TSH abnormal + FT4 abnormal
→ Hypothyroidism is likely.
Two of the three abnormal:
Possible:
Primary hypothyroidism
• ↑ TSH
• ↓ FT4
OR
Secondary hypothyroidism
• ↓ TSH
• ↓ FT4
Only one abnormal:
Do not immediately diagnose hypothyroidism.
Reevaluate the dog in 3–6 months.
FELINE HYPERTHYROIDISM
29. General Information
Hyperthyroidism is the most common endocrinopathy in cats.
The most common cause is:
Adenomatous hyperplasia of the thyroid gland
It usually affects:
• Middle-aged to older cats
There is:
• No major breed predisposition
• No major sex predisposition
Although the incidence is reported to be higher in purebred cats than mixed-breed
cats.
30. Dietary Iodine and Feline Hyperthyroidism
Commercial/balanced pet foods normally provide enough iodine.
However, the iodine content of foods can vary considerably.
Changes in dietary iodine have been studied as possible risk factors.
Important distinction:
Acute changes in dietary iodine can alter FT4.
However, with chronic changes, the thyroid usually adapts, so chronic dietary iodine
variation is unlikely by itself to explain feline hyperthyroidism.
31. Goitrogens and Feline Hyperthyroidism
Goitrogens can clearly cause hypothyroidism.
There has also been a theory that chronic exposure to goitrogens could eventually
contribute to toxic nodular goiter and hyperthyroidism.
This remains a proposed explanation rather than a clearly established cause.
32. Soy Isoflavones
Soy proteins contain isoflavones, including:
• Genistein
• Daidzein
These compounds have been found in many dry cat foods.
They have been suggested as possible thyroid-disrupting compounds because some
studies show that they can interfere with:
• Thyroperoxidase
• 5′-deiodinase
Study in cats
In a study of 18 healthy cats eating a soy diet containing 400 mg isoflavones/kg diet:
• TT4 increased modestly but significantly

-- 13 of 17 --

• FT4 increased modestly but significantly
• T3 did not change
However, human research has generally not shown harmful effects of soy isoflavones
on thyroid function when soy is part of a balanced diet with adequate iodine.
Important interpretation:
The evidence linking soy isoflavones to feline hyperthyroidism is inconsistent.
33. Canned Cat Food and BADGE
Canned cat foods have been associated with feline hyperthyroidism in several
epidemiological studies.
One suspected goitrogen is:
Bisphenol-A-diglycidyl ether (BADGE)
BADGE was used in the lining of easy-open pop-top cans.
The proposed mechanism is:
BADGE from can lining → possible leaching into food → ingestion by cat
Cats may be particularly susceptible because they have a reduced ability to detoxify
substances through hepatic glucuronidation.
Bisphenol A has also been shown in experimental studies to:
• Reduce T3 binding
• Increase TSH
• Cause hyperthyroidism/goiter in some experimental animals and some
humans
However, rodent studies suggest a large safety margin.
Important:
An epidemiological association does not prove cause and effect.
Although more than 90% of cats in the United States eat commercial pet food as
their main food source, only a relatively small proportion develop hyperthyroidism.
34. Pathology of Feline Hyperthyroidism
Feline hyperthyroidism resembles toxic nodular goiter in humans.
It is characterized by:
Autonomous growth of thyroid follicles
This means the thyroid tissue can grow and produce hormones without normal
dependence on TSH.
35. TSH Receptor and G-Protein Signaling
Normally:
TSH → TSH receptor → G protein → cAMP → thyroid hormone production + cell
growth
In toxic nodular goiter, abnormalities can occur in this signaling system.
Possible changes include:
• Increased stimulatory G-proteins
• Decreased inhibitory G-proteins
• Mutations in the TSH receptor that keep it activated even without TSH
Similar abnormalities have been investigated in hyperthyroid cats.
In some cats, activating mutations of the TSH receptor may contribute to disease.
Hyperthyroid cats may also have decreased expression of inhibitory G-proteins.
Important difference from human Graves disease:
Feline hyperthyroidism does not appear to be caused by stimulating antibodies
against the TSH receptor.
36. Environmental Risk Factors
Several environmental factors have been investigated.
Cat litter
Use of cat litter has been associated with increased hyperthyroidism risk.
However, this may simply reflect indoor living.
Indoor cats tend to:
• Live longer
• Therefore have more opportunity to develop hyperthyroidism
Pesticides and herbicides
These have been associated with thyroid abnormalities in other species.
Flea-control products
Some studies found an association between flea-control products and feline
hyperthyroidism.
However:
No specific product or ingredient was consistently identified as the cause.
37. Brominated Flame Retardants
Brominated flame retardants (BFRs) have also been proposed as possible
environmental goitrogens/carcinogens.
One group of interest is:
Polybrominated diphenyl ethers (PBDEs)
These compounds were detected in cats at serum levels reported to be 10–400 times
higher than levels found in humans.
Possible reasons include:
• Indoor living
• Frequent grooming
• Exposure to furniture and carpets
• Smaller body size
However, this remains a proposed association, not proof of causation.
CLINICAL SIGNS OF FELINE HYPERTHYROIDISM
38. Hypermetabolic Signs
Hyperthyroidism produces a state of hypermetabolism.

Common clinical signs include:
• Polyphagia
• Weight loss
• Vomiting
• Diarrhea
• Polydipsia
• Polyuria
A common pattern is:
Increased appetite + weight loss
39. Sympathetic Nervous System Signs
Hyperthyroidism also increases sympathetic activity.
Typical signs include:
• Hyperactivity
• Tachycardia
• Pupillary dilation
• Behavioral changes
40. Long-Term Complications
Long-standing hyperthyroidism can lead to:
Cardiovascular
• Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
• High-output heart failure
General
• Cachexia = severe loss of body condition/muscle
Severe chronic disease may eventually be fatal.
CLINICOPATHOLOGICAL FINDINGS
41. Erythrocytosis and Stress Leukogram
Hyperthyroid cats may develop:
Erythrocytosis
and a:
Stress leukogram
The stress leukogram may include:
• Neutrophilia
• Lymphocytosis
These changes are related to increased circulating catecholamines.
42. BUN and Creatinine
Increased breakdown of muscle may cause:
↑ BUN
without a similar increase in creatinine.
Why?
Hyperthyroidism also increases:
Glomerular filtration rate (GFR)
The increased GFR may temporarily mask underlying kidney disease.
Therefore, a seemingly normal creatinine does not necessarily mean that renal
function is normal.
43. Urinalysis
The effect on urinalysis is variable.
However, many hyperthyroid cats have:
↓ Urine specific gravity
especially when they have polyuria.
44. Liver Enzymes
The increased metabolic rate causes liver hypermetabolism.
As a result:
ALT and AST may be increased.
Increased liver enzyme activities occur in approximately:
80–90% of hyperthyroid cats
45. Cholesterol
Hyperthyroidism can cause:
↓ Serum cholesterol
This is mainly due to:
↑ Hepatic clearance of cholesterol
rather than reduced cholesterol synthesis.
DIAGNOSIS OF FELINE HYPERTHYROIDISM
46. Total T4
The usual initial diagnostic test is:
Total T4 (TT4)
An increased TT4 strongly supports hyperthyroidism in a compatible cat.
47. Free T4
As cats are diagnosed at earlier stages, FT4 can be particularly useful for detecting:
Early or occult hyperthyroidism
However, FT4 should generally be interpreted together with clinical findings and other
thyroid tests.
48. T3 Suppression Test
A T3 suppression test can help confirm hyperthyroidism in borderline cases.
In a normal cat:

-- 15 of 17 --

Administering T3 causes negative feedback:
Exogenous T3 → ↓ TSH → ↓ thyroid activity → ≥50% decrease in T4
So:
Normal cat → T4 decreases by at least 50%
In a hyperthyroid cat:
The thyroid gland functions more autonomously and does not respond normally to
the negative-feedback effect.
Therefore:
Hyperthyroid cat → T4 does not decrease appropriately
This makes the test useful for borderline cases.
49. Thyroid Scintigraphy
Thyroid scintigraphy can be used to visualize thyroid tissue that is hyperfunctional.
It helps identify areas of increased thyroid activity.
