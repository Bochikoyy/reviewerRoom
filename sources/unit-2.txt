THE HYPOTHALAMUS
1. General Function of the Hypothalamus
The hypothalamus is the major connection between the nervous system and
endocrine system.
It coordinates the activity of the pituitary gland by producing chemical messengers,
especially:
• Releasing hormones
• Other peptides
• Amines
These substances control pituitary hormone secretion.
The hypothalamus is also an important control center for many functions of the
autonomic nervous system.
2. Location and Structure of the Hypothalamus
The hypothalamus is part of the diencephalon.
It forms the floor of the third ventricle.
Important structures associated with the hypothalamus include:
• Optic chiasma
• Tuber cinereum
• Mammillary bodies
• Median eminence
The following structures are sometimes not included as part of the hypothalamus
itself, although they are actually extensions of it into the pituitary:
• Infundibulum
• Neurohypophysis
The neurohypophysis includes the stalk of the posterior pituitary and the posterior
lobe.
3. What Does the Hypothalamus Control?
The hypothalamus produces peptides and amines that influence the pituitary gland.
These signals can cause the pituitary to produce:
A. Tropic hormones
These hormones act on another endocrine gland and stimulate it to produce
hormones.
Example:
Hypothalamus → pituitary ACTH → adrenal cortex → cortisol
ACTH is therefore a tropic hormone because it stimulates another endocrine gland.
B. Hormones that directly produce a biological effect
Some pituitary hormones act more directly on tissues.
Example:
Prolactin (PRL) acts directly on tissues involved in milk production.
THE PITUITARY GLAND (HYPOPHYSIS)
4. Parts of the Pituitary Gland
The pituitary gland, also called the hypophysis, consists of four major parts:
1. Adenohypophysis – anterior pituitary
o Mainly the pars distalis
2. Neurohypophysis – posterior pituitary
o Mainly the pars nervosa
3. Pars intermedia – intermediate lobe
4. Pars tuberalis
5. Embryonic Origin of the Pituitary
The anterior and posterior pituitary have different embryonic origins.
Adenohypophysis
• Develops from an upward extension of the embryonic oral ectoderm.
• This structure is called Rathke's pouch.
Neurohypophysis
• Develops from a downward extension of neural ectoderm from the floor of
the third ventricle.
The two developing structures grow toward each other and form the pituitary gland.
Easy reminder:
Anterior pituitary → Rathke's pouch → oral ectoderm
Posterior pituitary → neural ectoderm → hypothalamus
THE NEUROHYPOPHYSIS / POSTERIOR PITUITARY
6. The Posterior Pituitary Is an Extension of the Hypothalamus
The neurohypophysis is different from the anterior pituitary because its nerve cell
bodies are actually located in the hypothalamus.
Most of these cell bodies are located in:
• Supraoptic nucleus
• Paraventricular nucleus
Their axons extend from the hypothalamus down into the posterior pituitary.
Structure:
Hypothalamus (cell bodies) → axons → pituitary stalk → posterior pituitary (nerve
endings)
Therefore:
Posterior pituitary = extension of the hypothalamus
7. Neurosecretory Neurons
The neurons that produce posterior pituitary hormones are called neurosecretory
neurons.
They are different from ordinary neurons.
Neurosecretory neurons:

-- 1 of 11 --

1. Do not innervate other neurons
o Although they themselves receive neural input.
2. Release their secretory products into the bloodstream.
3. Their hormones can travel to and act on distant tissues.
This makes them a direct connection between the nervous system and endocrine
system.
Another important difference:
Anterior pituitary hormones often stimulate other tissues or endocrine glands to
produce hormones.
Posterior pituitary hormones can directly cause the desired response in their target
tissues.
8. Main Posterior Pituitary Hormones
The two major hormones associated with the neurohypophysis are:
• Oxytocin
• Vasopressin, also called antidiuretic hormone (ADH)
Important point:
Although they are released from the posterior pituitary, they are actually synthesized
in the hypothalamus.
9. Synthesis and Transport of Oxytocin and Vasopressin
Both hormones are produced by magnocellular neurons.
"Magnocellular" means the neurons have large cell bodies.
Both oxytocin and vasopressin are produced in both the supraoptic and
paraventricular nuclei.
They are not strictly produced by only one nucleus.
10. Formation of Vasopressin
Vasopressin is first synthesized in the hypothalamic cell body as a preprohormone
called:
PrepropressoPhysin
The process is:
Preprohormone → removal of "pre" portion → prohormone → transported down
axon → further cleavage → vasopressin
A peptide fragment remaining after processing is called neurophysin II.
11. Formation of Oxytocin
Oxytocin is initially produced as the preprohormone:
Preprooxyphysin
The "pre" portion is removed, and the resulting prohormone is packaged into granules.
As the granules move down the axon, the prohormone is cleaved to produce:
Oxytocin + neurophysin I
The physiological function of the neurophysins is not currently known.
Additional point:
Neurophysin I is released into the blood along with oxytocin and can be measured as
an alternative indicator of oxytocin release.
12. Transport to the Posterior Pituitary
The hormones are made in the cell bodies in the hypothalamus.
The secretory granules are then transported down the axons to the posterior
pituitary.
There, the hormones are stored in the nerve terminals until a stimulus causes their
release.
Easy flow:
Hypothalamic cell body → hormone synthesis → secretory granules → axonal
transport → posterior pituitary → storage in nerve endings
13. Release of Oxytocin and Vasopressin
Hormone release begins when the hypothalamic neuron is stimulated by neural
input.
Sequence:
Neural stimulation
→ depolarization of neuron
→ action potential travels down the axon
→ reaches nerve terminal
→ Ca²⁺ enters the nerve terminal
→ secretory granules fuse with the membrane
→ exocytosis
→ oxytocin or vasopressin enters the bloodstream
Key requirement:
Calcium (Ca²⁺) is required for hormone release.
OXYTOCIN
14. Main Effects of Oxytocin
Oxytocin mainly causes smooth muscle contraction.
Its major targets include:
Mammary gland
It causes contraction of myoepithelial cells surrounding the mammary alveoli.
This pushes milk out of the alveoli and into the ducts and cisterns.
Suckling → hypothalamus → oxytocin → myoepithelial contraction → milk ejection
Uterus
Oxytocin causes contraction of the myometrium of the uterus.
Therefore, the major actions of oxytocin are:
Mammary gland → milk ejection

Uterus → smooth muscle contraction
VASOPRESSIN / ADH
15. Main Functions of Vasopressin
Vasopressin has two major actions:
Primary effect: Antidiuresis
Its most important function is to help the kidneys retain water.
This is why vasopressin is also called:
Antidiuretic hormone (ADH)
It is the most important hormone for controlling body water balance.
Secondary effect: Vasopressor action
Vasopressin can also cause contraction of vascular smooth muscle.
This increases blood pressure.
Easy reminder:
ADH → saves water
Vasopressin → also raises blood pressure
16. Forms of Vasopressin in Different Species
The major form differs among species:
Species Main hormone form
Most species Arginine vasopressin (AVP)
Pigs Lysine vasopressin
Birds Arginine vasotocin
CONTROL OF VASOPRESSIN SECRETION
17. Plasma Osmolality Controls ADH Release
The most important stimulus for vasopressin release is a change in plasma
osmolality.
Osmolality = concentration of dissolved substances in body fluids relative to
water.
When plasma osmolality increases:
This usually means there is too little water relative to solutes.
↑ Plasma osmolality
→ hypothalamic osmoreceptors increase their firing
→ stimulate vasopressin-producing neurons
→ ↑ vasopressin release
→ kidneys retain more water
→ body water increases
→ osmolality moves back toward normal
This is a negative-feedback system.
The body normally keeps the solute-to-water ratio within approximately 1–2% of
normal.
18. Other Receptors Involved in Water Balance
The body also has receptors in the:
• Esophagus
• Stomach
These can detect water intake very quickly.
This allows the body to respond to drinking even before major changes in plasma
osmolality occur.
19. Blood Volume Also Controls Vasopressin
Vasopressin secretion is also influenced by blood volume.
When blood volume decreases:
↓ Blood volume
→ stretch receptors in the atria detect the change
→ neural pathways alter inhibition of the osmoreceptor system
→ vasopressin release increases
→ kidney retains more water
→ blood volume is supported
Changes that reduce blood pressure also stimulate vasopressin release through:
• Baroreceptors in the carotid sinus
• Baroreceptors in the aortic arch
Important idea:
↑ Osmolality → ↑ ADH
↓ Blood volume → ↑ ADH
↓ Blood pressure → ↑ ADH
DIABETES INSIPIDUS (DI)
20. What Is Diabetes Insipidus?
Diabetes insipidus (DI) is a disorder in which the body cannot properly conserve
water because ADH is absent or ineffective.
The major signs are:
• Polyuria = excessive urine production
• Polydipsia = excessive thirst and water intake
• Dilute urine with low specific gravity or osmolality
21. Types of Diabetes Insipidus
There are two major types:
A. Central diabetes insipidus
The problem is insufficient ADH secretion.
There is an absolute or relative deficiency of circulating ADH.
Central DI may be:
• Primary

-- 3 of 11 --

o Idiopathic
o Congenital
• Secondary
o Commonly associated with head trauma
o Neoplasia can also cause it
B. Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus
ADH may be present, but the kidneys do not respond properly to it.
Simple comparison:
Central DI = not enough ADH
Nephrogenic DI = ADH present, but kidney cannot respond
22. Clinical Signs of Diabetes Insipidus in Dogs and Cats
Central DI can occur:
• At any age
• In any breed
• In either sex
However, young adults around 6 months of age are most commonly affected.
Major signs include:
• Profound polyuria
• Polydipsia
• Nocturia = urinating during the night
• Urinary incontinence
• Signs often present for several months
A commonly reported urine/water intake pattern is:
>100 mL/kg/day
compared with a normal range of approximately:
40–70 mL/kg/day
The severity depends on whether the ADH problem is partial or complete.
Other possible signs:
• Weight loss
• Dehydration
Animals may lose weight partly because they are constantly seeking water.
23. Laboratory Findings in DI
Routine tests are often surprisingly normal.
CBC and serum chemistry
Usually:
Normal
Plasma osmolality
Often high because of dehydration:
>310 mOsm/L
This can occur in both:
• Central DI
• Nephrogenic DI
Urinalysis
Usually unremarkable except for persistently dilute urine.
Typical urine specific gravity:
1.004–1.012
24. Primary Polydipsia vs DI
Primary polydipsia is excessive drinking that is not caused by central or nephrogenic
DI.
Because these animals drink too much water, they may become overhydrated.
Therefore:
Primary polydipsia → low plasma osmolality
Often:
<290 mOsm/L
This contrasts with DI, where plasma osmolality is often increased because of
dehydration.
25. Secondary Changes From Dehydration
If the animal is prevented from drinking enough water, dehydration may cause findings
such as:
• Slightly increased hematocrit
• Hypernatremia
These findings are usually secondary to dehydration, rather than being the primary
disorder.
26. Diagnosing Diabetes Insipidus
Tests used to distinguish:
• Central DI
• Nephrogenic DI
• Primary polydipsia
include:
A. Modified water deprivation test
This determines:
1. Whether the body releases endogenous ADH when dehydrated.
2. Whether the kidneys can respond to ADH.
B. ADH supplementation / response test
The animal's response to administered ADH can help distinguish whether the
problem is:
• Lack of ADH, or
• Failure of the kidneys to respond to ADH.

27. Important Safety Point About Water Deprivation Testing
Common causes of polyuria and polydipsia (PU/PD) should be ruled out before
performing a water deprivation test.
This is especially important because:
Undiagnosed renal failure + water deprivation → potentially serious illness
Failure to recognize renal failure beforehand may result in:
• An incorrect diagnosis
• An inconclusive test
• Significant morbidity
SIADH
28. Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion
SIADH occurs when vasopressin is released inappropriately, even though there is no
proper osmotic or blood-volume stimulus for its release.
In other words:
ADH is being secreted when the body does not actually need it.
Hypersecretion is rare.
29. Causes of SIADH
Neoplasia (tumors) is often associated with SIADH.
Some tumors produce ADH or ADH-like activity outside the normal hypothalamic-
pituitary system. These are called ectopic tumors.
The lungs are a common location for the tumors associated with SIADH.

THE ANTERIOR PITUITARY
1. Main Hormones of the Anterior Pituitary
The adenohypophysis (anterior pituitary) includes the:
• Pars distalis
• Pars intermedia
The six major hormones associated with the anterior pituitary are:
1. Growth hormone (GH) or somatotropin
2. Prolactin (PRL)
3. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) or thyrotropin

-- 5 of 11 --

4. Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)
5. Luteinizing hormone (LH)
6. Corticotropin (ACTH) or adrenocorticotropic hormone
2. Growth Hormone and Prolactin
GH and PRL are called somatomammotropins.
Growth hormone (GH)
• Produced by somatotropes
• Somatotropes are acidophilic cells
• GH is a single-chain protein
• Has two disulfide bonds
Prolactin (PRL)
• Produced by lactotropes
• PRL is also a single-chain protein
• Has three disulfide bonds
Relationship between GH and PRL
GH and PRL have some overlapping effects because about 50% of their amino acid
sequences are similar.
However:
GH is strongly species-specific in its biological activity.
This means GH from one species may not produce the same biological effects in
another species.
3. TSH, FSH, and LH
TSH, FSH, and LH are produced by:
• Thyrotropes → TSH
• Gonadotropes → FSH and LH
These three hormones are glycoproteins, meaning they contain carbohydrate
groups attached to the protein.
All three have:
• α (alpha) subunit
• β (beta) subunit
The two subunits are held together by noncovalent bonds.
Alpha subunit
• The same among TSH, FSH, and LH
• They are essentially interchangeable
Beta subunit
• Different for each hormone
• Determines the hormone's specific biological action
Easy rule:
α subunit = shared/common
β subunit = specific function
4. Other Glycoprotein Gonadotropins
Other hormones belong to the same general glycoprotein family, although they are not
produced by the anterior pituitary.
Examples include:
• Equine chorionic gonadotropin (eCG), formerly called pregnant mare's
serum gonadotropin
• Primate chorionic gonadotropin
These are produced by placental cells.
ACTH AND THE PROOPIOMELANOCORTIN FAMILY
5. ACTH Comes From a Common Prohormone
ACTH and β-lipotropin belong to the proopiomelanocortin (POMC) family.
This means both are made from the same large precursor molecule:
POMC → cleavage → ACTH and other peptides
POMC is produced in both:
• Pars distalis
• Pars intermedia
However, the final products are different in each region.
6. Pars Distalis
In the pars distalis:
POMC → ACTH
ACTH is produced by corticotropes.
ACTH then acts mainly on the adrenal cortex to stimulate production of adrenal
hormones, especially cortisol.
7. Pars Intermedia
In the pars intermedia:
ACTH → α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (α-MSH)
α-MSH is the predominant hormone of the pars intermedia.
Another peptide fragment is also produced:
Corticotropin-like intermediate lobe peptide
Its physiological function is not known.
8. β-Lipotropin
In both the pars distalis and pars intermedia:
β-lipotropin → β-endorphins + γ-lipotropin
β-Endorphins
• Have opioid activity
• May help regulate or modulate gonadotropin secretion

HYPOTHALAMIC CONTROL OF THE ANTERIOR PITUITARY
9. How the Hypothalamus Controls the Anterior Pituitary
For many years, scientists did not understand how the brain controlled the anterior
pituitary.
The key discovery was the hypothalamic-pituitary portal blood system.
Important historical discovery
In the 1930s, Popa and Fielding described the blood vessels connecting the
hypothalamus and pituitary.
Around 1950, Geoffrey Harris concluded that blood flows:
Hypothalamus → anterior pituitary
through a portal blood system.
10. Hypophyseal Portal System
The blood supply follows this general pathway:
Dorsal hypophyseal artery
→ capillary network in the median eminence
→ portal veins
→ capillary/sinusoidal network in the pars distalis
The dorsal hypophyseal artery supplies the adenohypophysis.
The ventral hypophyseal artery supplies the neurohypophysis.
The portal system has two major venous pathways supplying different areas of the
pars distalis:
• One supplies the ventral/central region
• One supplies the dorsal/peripheral region
Main purpose of the portal system
It allows hypothalamic hormones to travel directly to the anterior pituitary without
first being diluted throughout the general circulation.
11. Hypophysiotropic (Hypothalamic Releasing) Hormones
The hypothalamus produces regulatory hormones called hypophysiotropic
hormones.
These are released into the median eminence and then enter the portal blood
system.
They travel to the anterior pituitary and control the secretion of its hormones.
Basic pathway:
Hypothalamus → releasing/inhibiting hormone → portal blood → anterior pituitary
→ pituitary hormone
The production of these hypothalamic regulatory hormones is itself controlled by:
• Neural signals
• Hormonal signals
Some of these pituitary-related hormones are also found in other areas, including:
• Brain
• Gastrointestinal tract
• Pancreas

TROPIC HORMONES
14. Which Anterior Pituitary Hormones Are Tropic?
A tropic hormone mainly acts on another endocrine tissue and stimulates it to
produce hormones.
Originally, four anterior pituitary hormones were considered tropic:
• FSH
• LH
• TSH
• ACTH
GH is now also considered tropic
GH stimulates the liver to produce somatomedins, especially:
Insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1)
IGF-1 then contributes to the negative feedback regulation of GH.
PRL
PRL remains the main anterior pituitary hormone for which negative feedback from
its target tissue hormones has not been demonstrated.
FEEDBACK CONTROL OF THE ANTERIOR PITUITARY
15. Negative Feedback Is the Main Control Mechanism
The secretion of anterior pituitary protein hormones is mainly controlled by negative
feedback.
The target organ's hormone feeds back to reduce further stimulation from the
hypothalamus and/or pituitary.
16. Long-Loop Feedback
In a long-loop feedback system, the hormone produced by the target endocrine
organ feeds back to both:
• Hypothalamus
• Anterior pituitary
Example: ACTH and cortisol
Hypothalamus → CRH → anterior pituitary → ACTH → adrenal cortex → cortisol
Then:
Cortisol → inhibits CRH + inhibits ACTH
This prevents excessive cortisol production.
Easy flow:
CRH → ACTH → cortisol
Cortisol ─| CRH and ACTH
17. Short-Loop Feedback
A short-loop feedback system occurs when an anterior pituitary hormone directly
inhibits the hypothalamus.
Example:
ACTH ─| CRH
So:
Pituitary hormone → inhibits hypothalamic releasing hormone
18. Pulsatile Secretion of Gonadotropins
Negative feedback does not mean that pituitary hormones are released continuously
at a constant rate.
For example, even though estrogen continuously inhibits gonadotropin secretion,
FSH and LH are released in pulses.
The gonadotropins alternate between:
Secretion → no secretion → secretion → no secretion
The ovarian hormonal environment influences the:
• Pulse frequency
• Pulse amplitude
Progesterone-dominant state
• Lower pulse frequency
• Higher pulse amplitude
Estrogen-dominant state
• Higher pulse frequency
• Lower pulse amplitude
This demonstrates that hormone secretion is dynamic and pulsatile, rather than
constant.
CLINICAL APPLICATION: GROWTH HORMONE DISORDERS
19. Growth Hormone Deficiency: Pituitary Dwarfism
Pituitary dwarfism occurs when GH production is insufficient because of damage or
abnormal development of the pituitary gland.
Possible causes include:
• Neoplasia
• Degenerative disease
• Developmental abnormalities
Other pituitary hormones may also be decreased, including:
• TSH
• ACTH
• LH
• FSH
• GH
This condition may therefore result in panhypopituitarism when multiple pituitary
hormones are deficient.

20. Pituitary Dwarfism in Dogs
It is most commonly seen in:
German Shepherd dogs, usually 2–6 months old
Other reported breeds include:
• Carnelian bear dogs
• Spitz-type dogs
• Toy Pinschers
• Weimaraners
In German Shepherds, it is inherited as an:
Autosomal recessive trait
and is associated with a cystic Rathke's pouch.
21. Clinical Signs of Pituitary Dwarfism
The first signs usually appear during the first 2–3 months of life.
Major signs:
• Slow growth
• Proportionate dwarfism
• Mental retardation/developmental delay
• Difficulty with house training
Physical findings:
• Retained puppy hair coat
• Hypotonic skin
• Truncal alopecia (hair loss over the body)
• Hyperpigmentation
• Infantile genitalia
• Delayed dental eruption
22. Clinicopathologic Findings
Possible laboratory abnormalities include:
• Eosinophilia
• Lymphocytosis
• Mild normocytic, normochromic anemia
• Hypophosphatemia
• Sometimes hypoglycemia
Hypoglycemia may occur because of secondary adrenal insufficiency when ACTH is
also deficient.
23. Differential Diagnoses for Stunted Growth
Other causes of poor growth must be considered, including:
• Hypothyroid dwarfism
• Portosystemic shunt
• Diabetes mellitus
• Hyperadrenocorticism
• Malnutrition
• Parasitism
24. Diagnosis of Pituitary Dwarfism
Diagnosis may involve measuring:
• Serum GH
• IGF-1 (somatomedin C)
GH assays for dogs and cats are no longer commercially available in many settings.
Advantage of IGF-1
IGF-1 is:
• More stable
• Not species-specific
Therefore, it is useful for detecting GH-related disorders.
Affected dogs may also show subnormal responses to TSH and ACTH stimulation
tests.
Endogenous:
• TSH ↓
• ACTH ↓
This occurs because multiple anterior pituitary hormones may be deficient.
ACROMEGALY / HYPERSOMATOTROPISM
25. What Is Acromegaly?
Acromegaly is caused by chronic excessive GH secretion in an adult animal.
Unlike pituitary dwarfism:
Pituitary dwarfism = too little GH
Acromegaly = too much GH
26. Causes of Acromegaly
Dogs
Canine acromegaly is very rare.
It is usually associated with administration of progestational drugs to intact female
dogs to suppress estrus.
These hormones stimulate mammary tissue to produce excessive GH.
Cats
Feline acromegaly is usually caused by a:
GH-secreting anterior pituitary tumor
These tumors usually:
• Grow slowly
• May be present for a long time before obvious signs develop

-- 9 of 11 --

It is most common in:
• Older cats
• Approximately 8–14 years old
• More common in males
27. Common Clinical Signs of Acromegaly
The most common initial signs are often those of poorly controlled diabetes
mellitus:
• Polydipsia
• Polyuria
• Polyphagia
A very important clue is:
Weight gain despite uncontrolled diabetes
Animals may develop an increase in lean body mass, rather than the expected weight
loss associated with uncontrolled diabetes.
28. Organ Enlargement
Acromegaly can cause organomegaly.
Possible findings include:
• Renomegaly
• Hepatomegaly
• Enlargement of endocrine organs
29. Physical Changes
Some affected dogs and cats may develop enlargement of:
• Paws/extremities
• Jaw
• Tongue
• Forehead
• Skull
There may also be:
• Increased muscle mass
• Growth of the acral portions of the body
Acral areas include the:
• Paws
• Chin
• Skull
30. Cardiovascular and Renal Changes
These usually develop later in the disease.
Cardiovascular
• Cardiomegaly
• Systolic murmurs
• Congestive heart failure
Renal
Approximately 50% of acromegalic cats may eventually develop azotemia.
31. Neurologic Signs
Neurological signs commonly described in human acromegaly are generally not seen
in small animals.
Examples include:
• Peripheral neuropathy
• Paresthesias
• Carpal tunnel syndrome
• Sensory and motor defects
• Headache
• Visual field abnormalities
These are generally not typical findings in acromegalic dogs and cats.
32. Acromegaly and Diabetes Mellitus
Insulin resistance and impaired glucose tolerance occur in essentially all cats and
most dogs with acromegaly.
This leads to:
↑ Insulin resistance → ↑ blood glucose → diabetes mellitus
Despite this severe insulin resistance:
• Serum insulin concentrations are very high
• Ketosis is uncommon
Important clinical clue
Suspect acromegaly in a diabetic cat, especially a male cat, when the diabetes is
extremely insulin-resistant.
33. Diagnosis of Acromegaly
Definitive diagnosis requires evidence of increased:
• GH, or
• IGF-1
Because canine and feline GH assays are no longer commercially available in many
settings, IGF-1 is commonly used.
IGF-1 is useful because it is:
• Non-species-specific
• More practical for testing
Imaging
In cats, the most definitive approach combines:
↑ Serum IGF-1 + CT or MRI of the pituitary region

The diagnosis should also consider and exclude other causes of insulin resistance,
such as:
• Hyperthyroidism
• Hyperadrenocorticism
34. Important Frequency in Diabetic Cats
Acromegaly should be strongly considered in diabetic cats that fail to achieve
remission despite appropriate treatment.
The notes report that approximately 30% of diabetic cats that do not go into
remission with an ultra-low-carbohydrate diet and insulin therapy may be
acromegalic.
